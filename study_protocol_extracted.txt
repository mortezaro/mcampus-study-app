g. Summary of project (max. 1ʼ000 characters)  
This project investigates how attention and thinking ability fluctuate in everyday life and 
whether short speech samples and simple smartphone-based context signals can predict 
these fluctuations. Healthy adults (18–70 years) complete a baseline cognitive session and 
then brief sessions (5–7 minutes) on their own smartphones three times per day over 12 days. 
Each session includes a short state/context questionnaire (e.g. stress, fatigue, mood, location 
type, perceived noise), a 60–90 second speech prompt (picture description and 
semi-structured QA/narrative), and validated TestMyBrain (TMB) cognitive tasks. 
 Smartphone-based measures (e.g. device-relative ambient noise proxy, interruptions, device 
metadata) are recorded alongside performance. The study is fully remote, observational, and

contextual signals improve prediction of momentary cognitive performance beyond simple 
baselines.

Protocol  
 
Administrative information  
Title: A within-subject mobile study testing whether speech and smartphone-captured context predict momentary 
cognitive performance, using cognitive tasks in naturalistic environments. 
Structured summary: This is a fully remote, 12-day, within-subject trial delivered on participants’ own 
smartphones. Each day, three short sessions (morning, afternoon, evening) are completed in naturalistic settings. 
Each session consists of: (1) a brief state/context survey, (2) a 60–90 s speech prompt via the study web app, (3) 
short-form TestMyBrain (TMB) cognitive tasks, and (4) a feedback item. 
The primary objective is to test whether speech features can be used to predict momentary cognitive 
performance, specifically task accuracy and reaction time (RT). Predictors include speech features and 
smartphone/context signals (ambient noise proxy, interruptions, device type, headphone use, self-reported stress, 
fatigue, time of day).

Introduction  
Background & rationale: Intensive longitudinal work shows substantial within-person variability in cognition 
over short time scales; key drivers include time of day and internal/external states. Single snapshots can mislead. 
Delivering tasks on smartphones in naturalistic contexts lets us both measure performance and observe context 
that predicts it (noise, interruptions), while speech may index these states in real time. TMB provides validated, 
mobile-ready tasks and trial-level data suitable for unsupervised testing 
  
Objectives: 
1. Primary: Predict momentary cognitive performance (accuracy, RT) from concurrent speech features, 
beyond age, person-mean baseline, time of day, and self-reported states. 
2. Decompose trait (between-person) vs state (within-person) variance in performance, speech, and context 
indices. 
3. Quantify how smartphone/context signals (ambient noise proxy, interruptions, location type, 
stress/fatigue) predict momentary cognition and interact with speech. 
4. Compare global vs person-specific predictive models. 
Co-primary predictive endpoint: Adding speech features improves out-of-sample prediction of momentary 
performance over a baseline without speech (median ΔRMSE ≥3% for RT or ΔAUC ≥0.03 for accuracy; blocked 
CV by day).

Trial design 
Within-subject design across 12 days (3 sessions/day; morning/afternoon/evening windows). Each daily session 
follows a fixed sequence: (1) brief state/context survey, (2) 60–90 s speech prompt via the study web app to elicit 
a verbal response (picture description and QA/narrative prompt), (3) short-form TMB cognitive tasks, and (4) 
AE/feedback item. Thus, these tasks are administered three times per day during Days 1–12.

work, transit—if safe). 
Eligibility: 18–70; fluent in study language (English); smartphone with mic; internet; consent. 
Exclusion: Neurological disorders; major uncorrected speech/hearing impairment; acute psychiatric crisis.

Statistical methods: 
● Preprocessing: Exclude failed-QC sessions; winsorize RTs within participant; log-RT; standardize 
features within folds; impute missing self-reports within folds. 
● Variance decomposition: Multilevel models (random intercepts by participant; random slopes for time 
of day/context) to estimate trait vs state variance components (ICCs) for performance, speech, and 
context indices. 
● Primary (concurrent prediction): 
○ GLMM (accuracy) / LMM (log-RT): age + person-mean performance + time of day + 
self-reports + device + ambient noise proxy + context interruptions + speech feature vector. 
○ Evidence: Δ marginal R² when adding speech (and context) to a baseline model; LR tests. 
● Predictive (co-primary): 
○ Blocked CV by day. Compare M0 (age + person-mean) vs M1 (M0 + states + context) vs M2 
(M1 + speech). 
○ Metrics: RT → RMSE/MAE; Accuracy → AUC/Brier + calibration (ECE). 
○ Success: median ΔRMSE ≥3% or ΔAUC ≥0.03 (M1→M2) with bootstrap 95% CI > 0. 
● Context effects: Quantify unique contributions of ambient noise proxy and interruptions (and 
interactions with speech).

Appendix A: QA/narrative prompts 
A. Subjective Cognitive Functioning / Mental Clarity 
1. Describe situations in your everyday life when your thinking tends to feel especially clear or sharp. What 
are you usually doing, and what do you think helps? 
2. Describe some situations in your daily life when your thinking tends to feel slower or less clear. What 
kinds of activities are you usually doing, and how do you usually respond? 
3. Tell us about the kinds of things that usually make it harder for you to concentrate, and how you typically 
handle that. 
4. Think of a type of task that you generally find mentally demanding. Walk us through what it usually 
involves and how it typically feels for you. 
 
B. Fatigue, Effort, and Performance 
1. Describe what it is generally like for you when you feel mentally tired but continue with your activities. 
What kinds of things do you usually do in those situations? 
2. In a typical day, when do you tend to feel most alert and when do you tend to feel more tired? What are 
you usually doing at those times? 
3. Tell us about types of tasks that usually require more mental effort for you than you expect. What tends 
to make them feel harder, and how do you typically adapt? 
 
C. Attention, Interruptions, and Environment

and what usually happens afterwards. 
3. Describe situations in your daily life when you need to switch quickly from one task to another. What 
kinds of tasks are involved, and how does that switch usually feel for you? 
4. Describe a place where you often do things that require concentration (for example, where you usually 
study, work, or focus on tasks). What, if anything, makes it easier or harder to concentrate there?

usually work for you.
